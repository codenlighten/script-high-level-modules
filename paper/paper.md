# Witness-Assisted Pairing Cryptography on a Bitcoin Stack Machine

### BLS12-381, Groth16 verification, and an inverted cryptographic cost model

**Status: preprint draft.** Every number in [tables.md](tables.md) is generated
from `results.json`, which is generated by measuring the code. Prose is written
by hand and can be wrong; the tables cannot silently describe a version of the
implementation that no longer exists.

---

## Abstract

We investigate whether modern pairing-based cryptography can be expressed
directly in Bitcoin Script, and what it costs when it is. We compile the
BLS12-381 field tower, G2 curve arithmetic, the Miller loop, the final
exponentiation, multi-pairing products, subgroup membership tests and Groth16
verification into Bitcoin locking predicates built from ordinary opcodes —
`OP_MUL`, `OP_MOD`, `OP_ADD`, `OP_PICK`, `OP_ROLL`, `OP_WITHIN` — with no pairing
primitive, no extension-field type, and no arithmetic opcode beyond
arbitrary-precision integers.

Expensive intermediate values, principally field inversions, are supplied by the
spender and verified algebraically: `z = a⁻¹ (mod p)` becomes the constraint
`a·z ≡ 1 (mod p)`, with `z` bounded into `[0, p)` so that no second witness
encodes the same value.

**A complete BLS12-381 pairing was evaluated on Bitcoin SV mainnet.** Its two
stages were first deployed and spent separately — the 63-round Miller loop as a
333,676-byte locking script and the final exponentiation as a 474,207-byte one —
and then composed: a single transaction whose two inputs are the Miller loop and
the final exponentiation, bound to each other by a shared output commitment, so
that the network establishes the whole computation rather than its halves.

The second was made possible by a 2/6 compressed representation of the
cyclotomic subgroup whose squaring is closed by construction, with a
decompression derived from f·f̄ = 1 as two linear equations over Fp2. It reduces
the final exponentiation from 592,008 to 473,466 bytes, across the default
500,000-byte script policy boundary.

What has not executed on chain is a pairing in a *single script*: 817,031 bytes
against a 500,000-byte policy. The composition moved into the transaction
instead (§8.2), which is what made the result reachable at all.

We construct a Groth16 verification predicate of 1,246,986 bytes and 786,448
opcodes that checks each proof point into its prime-order subgroup, accepts a
valid proof, and rejects invalid proofs whose curve membership, subgroup
membership and inversion witnesses remain valid, isolating failure to the pairing
equation itself. Subgroup membership is decided by endomorphism — φ(P) = [−x²]P
on G1, ψ(Q) = [x]Q on G2 — for 17,790 bytes, 1.4% of the verifier; the G2 test
is nearly free because a Miller loop over Q has already computed [|x|]Q by the
time it ends (§7.6). Multi-pairing compilation reduces size by sharing the Miller
accumulator, saving 609,794 bytes for two pairings and 1,223,178 for three
relative to independent evaluation.

The transaction-level decomposition generalises past computations that happen to
have a seam. We cut the Groth16 verifier itself into three stages across three
inputs of one transaction (§8.3) — which forces a cut *inside* the Miller loop,
at round 31 of 63, because three pairings sharing one accumulator are 705,838
bytes of loop alone, already over the policy before the final exponentiation is
considered. The cut point is chosen by the size of the state it exposes, 2,156
bytes for a computation of 1,246,986, rather than by any structure in the
mathematics. Its three coins are funded on mainnet in block 966,795 and the 1,291,329-byte
transaction that spends them is mined in block 966,923 — but only after the relay
path refused it for exceeding a per-transaction validation-time budget and the
pool that mined it took it by direct submission (§8.3).

Correctness is established by differential testing against an independent
BLS12-381 implementation on all twelve Fp12 coefficients, adversarial mutation
of every witnessed value, separation of each module's specification from its
emitted schedule, randomised testing over each module's declared domain, and
byte-exact reconstruction of every deployed script from source. The Groth16
verifier additionally accepts a proof generated by an independent proving stack
(snarkjs), and refuses that same proof when built for a different public
statement. In the course of this work the test kit was found to have been
reporting as *refused* any refusal case whose spend it could not build — which
included every refusal case of the whole Groth16 verifier. They have all since
been run by the interpreter, and all still refuse (§10.4).

Finally, we identify an **inverted cost model**: because a witnessed inversion
costs roughly four multiplications to verify rather than hundreds to compute,
affine coordinates outperform the projective coordinates that conventional
elliptic-curve implementation practice prescribes — and compressed cyclotomic
squaring, whose decompression costs an inversion and which is therefore normally
reserved for long squaring chains, becomes worth taking at once. Implementation
strategies optimal for processors are not necessarily optimal for
witness-assisted verification, and the same reversal appears in more than one
place.

## 1. Introduction

Bitcoin Script is usually described as a deliberately limited language for
expressing payment conditions. After the Genesis upgrade, Bitcoin SV's
interpreter admits arbitrary-precision integer arithmetic, and the question of
what can be *expressed* separates from the question of what is *conventional*.

This paper reports an empirical answer to a narrow version of that question:
how much of a bilinear pairing can be written as a Bitcoin locking script, and
what does it cost?

The contributions are:

1. **A compilation path** from BLS12-381 pairing arithmetic to Bitcoin Script
   primitives, through a typed, stack-tracking assembler with per-module
   pre- and post-conditions (§4–§6).
2. **A witness-assisted arithmetic model** in which the spender computes
   expensive values and the script verifies algebraic identities, with the
   canonicity obligation that makes this sound (§3).
3. **Empirical mainnet execution of both stages** of a pairing, as two
   transactions, with byte-exact reconstruction from source (§8). The final
   exponentiation reaches the network only because of the compression in §6.1.
4. **A multi-pairing result**: k pairings sharing one Miller accumulator, with
   measured savings (§7).
5. **A Groth16 verifier** and the precise separation between the values a
   spender may choose and the values the locking script fixes (§7.2).
6. **Subgroup membership by endomorphism**, with every fact the tests depend on
   computed rather than recalled, and the G2 test taken from the running point
   the Miller loop already produces (§7.6).
7. **Transaction-level decomposition.** A computation exceeding the script-size
   policy is evaluated across two inputs of one spend, with the transaction
   enforcing equality of the intermediate state. Given a function f = F ∘ M and
   a commitment C to the value passing between them, the technique replaces one
   predicate too large to relay with two that are not:

   ```
   input 0:  P₁(x, s) = [ s = M(x) ] ∧ C(s)
   input 1:  P₂(s, y) = [ y = F(s) ] ∧ C(s)
   ```

   Both inputs observe the same commitment, so s is common to them, and the
   spend establishes y = F(M(x)). This is not specific to pairings: any
   computation with a small cut point admits it (§8.2).
8. **An inverted cost model** for elliptic-curve arithmetic under witness-
   assisted verification, and a mechanism that discovers it rather than
   asserting it (§9, §9.1).

We are explicit about what has *not* been shown. **A complete pairing has not
executed as a single locking script**, and cannot at 817,031 bytes under a
500,000-byte relay policy. It has executed as a transaction-level composition of
two independently verified scripts whose intermediate Fp12 state is bound by a
shared output commitment (§8.2) — a weaker statement about script size and an
identical one about what was computed. The Groth16 construction verifies a
statement fixed at locking time; a verifier accepting public inputs at spend
time requires on-chain scalar multiplication we price but do not emit.

## 2. Background

### 2.1 The interpreter

Post-Genesis, script numbers are arbitrary precision up to 750,000 bytes and
`OP_MUL` and `OP_MOD` operate at 4,096 bits. Every property this work depends on
was measured rather than quoted, as passing probes.

Three behaviours shape everything below:

- **`OP_MOD` is truncated, not Euclidean.** `(−3) mod 5` is `−3`. A value that
  might be negative when it reaches `OP_MOD` must have the modulus added first,
  and this determines the schedule of nearly every module in §5.
- **`OP_NUM2BIN` writes a signed number**, so a high bit becomes a sign bit.
- **The stack is capped at 1000 elements**, which bounds the working set rather
  than the script.

### 2.2 BLS12-381 and the tower

BLS12-381 is a pairing-friendly curve with a 381-bit base field. The pairing
e : G₁ × G₂ → G_T satisfies e(aP, bQ) = e(P, Q)^{ab}, and is computed in two
stages: a **Miller loop** over the 63-bit curve parameter, then a **final
exponentiation** to (p¹² − 1)/r.

The field tower is
Fp2 = Fp[u]/(u² + 1), Fp6 = Fp2[v]/(v³ − ξ) with ξ = u + 1, and
Fp12 = Fp6[w]/(w² − v).

Because p ≡ 3 (mod 4), the Fp2 Frobenius is conjugation — a single negation —
which is why a degree-12 Frobenius costs seven multiplications rather than a
381-bit exponentiation (§5.4).

G1 is the order-r subgroup of E(Fp) : y² = x³ + 4, and G2 the order-r subgroup of
the D-type sextic twist E′(Fp2) : y² = x³ + 4(u + 1). Neither is the whole curve:
#E(Fp) = h₁·r and #E′(Fp2) = h₂·r with cofactors h₁ and h₂ greater than one.

## 3. The witness-assisted model

### 3.1 The transformation

Computing a modular inverse in Script would require an extended Euclidean
algorithm or an exponentiation by p − 2. Instead the spender supplies `z` and
the script checks

```
a · z ≡ 1 (mod p)
```

which is one multiplication, one reduction and one comparison. In Fp2 it is one
Fp2 multiplication written out — four Fp multiplications and two comparisons.

Generally: **verification can be asymptotically and practically cheaper than
recomputation**, and a locking script is a verifier.

### 3.2 The obligation this creates

A witnessed value imposes two duties, and the second is where the bugs are.

**Soundness** — no *wrong* witness is accepted. `a · z ≡ 1` establishes this:
there is exactly one inverse.

**Canonicity** — no *second* witness is accepted either. Without a range check,
`z` and `z + p` both satisfy `a·z ≡ 1 (mod p)`, so a downstream comparison would
be comparing numbers that are congruent rather than equal. Every witnessed value
in this work is bounded into `[0, p)` by `OP_WITHIN`.

Four bugs of exactly this kind were found by systematic audit rather than by
testing, by asking of every module: *what can a spender choose, and is each
choice pinned to exactly one value?* Three were in code whose tests passed.

### 3.3 Testing witnesses adversarially

Every witnessed input is attacked on every run: near misses, off-by-p, sign
flips, zero, the modulus itself. The suite reports 1,367 forgery attempts, all
refused. This is separate from testing that correct inputs produce correct
outputs, and it catches a different class of defect.

## 4. The compilation path

```
high-level algorithm
      ↓  a JavaScript model — the specification, never the same code
field-operation schedule
      ↓  a typed, stack-tracking assembler
witness assignment
      ↓  hint(), replaying the algorithm to produce the spender's values
Bitcoin Script
      ↓  bsv.Script.Interpreter, under relay policy flags
accept / reject
```

Each module carries a `model` (what the answer is), an `emit` (how the script
computes it), `requires`/`ensures` contracts as intervals, and `cases`. The
model and the emitter are deliberately different code: a schedule and its
specification must be able to disagree, or the test is vacuous.

Contracts propagate. `fp2.mul` requires its inputs reduced into `[0, p)`; when
they are outputs of another module whose `ensures` said so, the requirement is
discharged and no check is emitted. When it is not discharged, a bound is
emitted. When it can be neither, the build fails. That third outcome is the
mechanism's purpose.

## 4.1 Three sizes, named once

A module has three honest sizes and the paper reports all three, so that Table 2
and Table 5 are read as an explanation rather than a contradiction.

| | what it is |
| --- | --- |
| **core** | the cryptographic computation, emitted alone |
| **standalone** | the deployable coin: core, plus an owner check and the comparison that makes it spendable |
| **composed** | the transaction-linked predicate: core, plus the covenant that publishes or consumes the intermediate state |

| | core | standalone | composed |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Miller loop | 332,977 | 333,676 | 343,799 |

The differences are 699 bytes of coin around the Miller loop and 10,822 of

size, which is the one that is a property of the arithmetic rather than of how
the arithmetic was wrapped.

## 5. Field arithmetic

See **Table 1** for measured sizes.

### 5.1 Fp2 multiplication, and why the products stay unreduced

Karatsuba gives r₀ = t₀ − t₁ and r₁ = t₂ − t₀ − t₁ from three products. Leaving
t₀ and t₁ *unreduced* makes the imaginary part identically a₀b₁ + a₁b₀ — a sum
of two non-negative numbers — so it needs no correction before a truncated
`OP_MOD`. Only the real part is a genuine difference and pays for one reduction
and one addition of p. Reducing t₀ and t₁ up front instead costs two extra
`OP_MOD`s *and* still needs a 2p correction, because the reduced difference no
longer carries the identity that makes it positive.

### 5.2 Cyclotomic squaring, and the basis that hid

After the easy part of the final exponentiation the accumulator lies in
G_Φ6(Fp2), where Granger–Scott squaring is available. Two implementations
written from the nested Fp6[w] indexing disagreed with general squaring and were
discarded.

The third works because of a change of *presentation*, not of algebra:
w² = v and w⁶ = ξ mean Fp12 is equally Fp2[w]/(w⁶ − ξ) with basis 1, w, …, w⁵,
and since (w³)² = ξ, the element is three Fp4 coefficients — **(g₀,g₃), (g₁,g₄),
(g₂,g₅)**. Those pairs are what the nested indexing conceals, and both failures
had them wrong.

### 5.3 Sparse line multiplication

Nine of a Miller line's twelve Fp2 coefficients are zero. On a processor the
zeros are skipped at negligible cost; in a locking script a multiplication is
*bytes*, present whether or not it does anything. Writing the product against
the three live coefficients takes it from eighteen Fp2 multiplications to
fourteen.

### 5.4 Frobenius

f ↦ f^p is six conjugations and seven multiplications by derived constants. The
module's *model* is the literal exponentiation f^p, so what is proven is that the
shortcut equals the thing it is a shortcut for, rather than equalling another
copy of itself.

## 6. The Miller loop

The loop over |x| = 0xd201000000010000 performs 63 squarings, 63 tangent steps,
5 chord steps and 68 line products. Two implementation points matter.

**The squaring belongs to the bit, not to the line.** A set bit contributes two
lines and still one square. Squaring before the chord yields f² where f was
wanted — a wrong answer costing 2,301 extra bytes to reach, which every
component test tolerated.

**λ is computed once.** The obvious factoring derives the line from the slope
and then asks a general point-addition routine for the next point, which
recomputes the slope: a second inversion for a number already in hand. Deriving
the next point from λ directly gives **68 inversions rather than 136**, halving
the witness.

### 6.0 The final exponentiation as an addition chain

λ = 3(p⁴ − p² + 1)/r is 1,270 bits, and raising to it directly is 1,270
squarings. Two structural facts collapse it. p is a Frobenius map rather than an
exponentiation, so λ written in base p becomes four digits applied by φ; and
every one of those digits is a small polynomial in the 63-bit curve parameter
y = |x|, with coefficients no larger than 3. What remains is **5 shared
ladders** — one exponentiation by y per power of y the digits need, reused
across all four base-p digits — and a handful of small multiplications.

1,270 squarings become 887 with the base-p step alone, and 315 with both. The
digits are derived at load time by the same arithmetic everything else uses, so
a mistranscribed constant cannot occur; an earlier chain written from memory was
wrong in a way that still produced an order-r element, which is to say it looked
right.

### 6.1 Compressed squaring, and a second inverted trade

Read the Granger–Scott identities in the Fp4 basis — f = c₀ + c₁w + c₂w² over
Fp4 = Fp2[s]/(s² − ξ) with s = w³ — and c₁′ and c₂′ depend only on c₁ and c₂.
So {c₁, c₂} is closed under squaring, c₀ can be dropped, and squaring costs two
Fp4 squarings instead of three. That is a 2/6 compression obtained by *reading*
formulas already verified rather than by deriving new ones.

Compression is only useful if one can come back, and decompression is where the
technique normally stops paying: it costs a field inversion, so it is reserved
for long runs of squarings. Under witness-assisted verification the inversion is
four multiplications, and the break-even moves down far enough that it is worth
taking immediately.

The recovery is derived rather than recalled. For f in the cyclotomic subgroup
f·f̄ = 1, and with f̄ = c̄₀ − c̄₁w + c̄₂w² two of the three resulting Fp4 equations
are **linear** in c₀ = x + ys:

```
w²:  2(c₀c̄₂)₀ = N(c₁)      ⟹   c₂ᵣ·x − ξc₂ₛ·y  =  N(c₁)/2
w¹:  2(c₁c̄₀)₁ = −N(c₂)     ⟹   c₁ₛ·x −   c₁ᵣ·y = −N(c₂)/2
```

writing u = uᵣ + uₛs and N(u) = uᵣ² − ξuₛ². A 2×2 solve over Fp2, one witnessed
inversion of the determinant, and both equations scaled by two so nothing is
halved.

| | bytes |
| --- | ---: |
| `fp12.powX`, uncompressed ladder | 98,902 |
| `fp12.powXc`, compressed | 73,582 |
| final exponentiation, before | 592,008 |
| final exponentiation, after | **473,466** |
| e(P, Q), before | 935,334 |
| e(P, Q), after | 817,031 |

25.6% off a ladder, 20.0% off the final exponentiation, 12.6% off a pairing —
and the exponentiation crosses the 500,000-byte policy boundary — which is why
it is on mainnet and not only in the interpreter.

The determinant ξ·c₁ₛc₂ₛ − c₁ᵣc₂ᵣ can vanish, and then nothing is recovered.
That is a **completeness** limit, not a soundness one: it cannot make a wrong
pairing verify, only a right one fail, and the values it depends on are
determined by the computation rather than chosen by the spender.

`fp12.powXc` is checked against the literal f^|x| by general exponentiation —
the same model `fp12.powX` carries — so the compressed schedule is verified
against a specification no part of it produced.

## 7. Products of pairings, and Groth16

### 7.1 Sharing the accumulator

Because the squaring belongs to the bit, k pairings evaluated on one accumulator
pay for 63 squarings between them and **one** final exponentiation. See
**Table 3**: each pairing after the first costs about 204,000 bytes rather than
817,000.

### 7.2 Binding the verification key to the predicate

Groth16 verification is

```
e(A, B) · e(−L, γ) · e(−C, δ) = e(α, β)
```

with L = IC₀ + Σ xᵢ·ICᵢ. **The soundness is not the arithmetic; it is who
chooses what.** Only A, B and C come from the unlocking script. γ, δ and L are
pushed as constants by the locking script, and e(α, β) is the constant they are
compared against.

A predicate taking all three pairs as inputs is a check of the *equation* and
not a verifier: a spender free to choose γ can choose one satisfying the equation
for a proof of nothing. The two constructions differ by 490 bytes of pushed
constant, and the test suite asserts the separation on every run.

### 7.3 The statement is the UTXO

L is a constant because the statement is fixed when the coin is locked. A
covenant that pays out to whoever proves *this* knows its public inputs when it
is written, so L is computed off chain once and pushed as 96 bytes rather than
costing about 40,000 bytes of on-chain scalar multiplication per input.

This is a structural fit rather than an optimisation. In a general verifier the
statement arrives with the proof; in a UTXO the statement *is* the output. A
verifier accepting public inputs at spend time is a different object and does
need the multiplications.

### 7.4 Fault-isolation testing

The negative cases are constructed so that failure can only be attributed to the
pairing equation. For an A and a C each displaced by one generator:

```
CurveValid = 1 ∧ SubgroupValid = 1 ∧ WitnessValid = 1 ∧ FieldArithmeticValid = 1
                ∧ Groth16Equation = 0   ⟹  Script = FALSE
```

Every point remains on the curve and in its subgroup; every witnessed inverse
remains correct for the points supplied, because the witness generator
recomputes them. Nothing fails until the equation does. A verifier that rejects
invalid proofs because they are malformed has not been shown to check the
intended relation.

### 7.5 A proof from an independent proving stack

The fixture above is constructed from the group law, which establishes that the
verifier checks the intended relation and says nothing about whether that
relation is the one a real prover produces. So the verifier is also run against
a proof **snarkjs** generated over BLS12-381 — its own trusted setup, its own
prover, its own field arithmetic — for the circuit c = a·b with c public
(a = 3, b = 11, so the public signal is 33).

Two things had to agree before the Script test meant anything, and neither was
guaranteed. snarkjs's G2 points are `[[x₀,x₁],[y₀,y₁]]` in the same Fp2 basis
used here, with no conjugation and no swap — checked by putting the swapped
reading on the twist, where it does not lie. And snarkjs checks
e(−A,B)·e(α,β)·e(L,γ)·e(C,δ) = 1 where this checks
e(A,B)·e(−L,γ)·e(−C,δ) = e(α,β); they are the same equation rearranged, and the
proof satisfies it under this implementation's pairing.

The locking script accepts the proof. It refuses a displaced one, points off
their curves, and points on their curves outside their subgroups — and, the case
worth having, **it refuses the same valid proof when the verifier is built for a
different public input**. L is a compile-time constant, so a different statement
is a different locking script: the statement is bound into the coin rather than
supplied alongside the proof. A verifier that accepted a valid proof of something
else would be checking arithmetic and not checking a claim.

A second snarkjs circuit asserts *"the holder was at least 21 years old as of
2026"* with the birth year private. Its proof is accepted; a proof generated from
an underage witness — snarkjs's prover does not check constraints, so one can be
produced — is refused; and the valid proof is refused by a coin that asks for 30.

`npm run groth16:external` and `npm run demo`; the vectors are in
`test/vectors/groth16-bls12381/` and `test/vectors/groth16-age/` with the scripts
that produced them.

### 7.6 Subgroup membership

A proof point that satisfies its curve equation need not lie in the order-r
subgroup. E(Fp) has h₁·r points and E′(Fp2) has h₂·r, and outside the subgroups
the pairing is not the bilinear map Groth16's soundness argument concerns. A
standard verifier checks membership; until this revision this one did not, and
§10.3 recorded that as its most significant open finding.

The direct test is [r]P = O, a 255-bit ladder. Each curve has an endomorphism
that acts on its subgroup as a small power of x, and comparing the two decides
membership for much less:

```
G1   φ(x, y) = (βx, y),  β³ = 1        P ∈ G1  ⟺  P ∈ E(Fp)  ∧ φ(P) = [−x²]P
G2   ψ = untwist ∘ π_p ∘ twist          Q ∈ G2  ⟺  Q ∈ E′(Fp2) ∧ ψ(Q) = [x]Q
```

Endomorphism tests for BLS12-381 are due to Bowe [12], and these two forms to
Scott [13]. They are not taken from either here. Each "⟸" is a short argument,
and each argument rests on facts about this curve that are easy to state and
easy to misremember, so `npm run subgroup` computes every one:

**G1.** φ³ = 1 and φ ≠ 1, so φ² + φ + 1 = 0 as an endomorphism. If
φ(P) = [−x²]P then O = (φ² + φ + 1)P = [x⁴ − x² + 1]P = [r]P, and since r ∤ h₁
the points of E(Fp) killed by r are exactly G1. The tool checks r = x⁴ − x² + 1,
#E(Fp) = p + 1 − t = h₁·r with t = x + 1, that φ² + φ + 1 = O on points *outside*
G1, and which cube root β is — both are tried against the generator, one acts as
[−x²] and the other as [x² − 1], the two roots of t² + t + 1 modulo r.

**G2.** ψ satisfies Frobenius's characteristic polynomial ψ² − tψ + p = 0. If
ψ(Q) = [x]Q then O = [x² − tx + p]Q = [p − x]Q, and p − x = h₁·r. Q also has
[h₂·r]Q = O; gcd(h₁, h₂) = 1 gives [r]Q = O; and r ∤ h₂. The tool derives ψ's two
coefficients from the untwisting map — ξ^((1−p)/3) and ξ^((1−p)/2), because the
D-type twist untwists by division — checks the characteristic polynomial on twist
points outside G2, and does not assume h₂: a sextic twist has one of six possible
orders, all six are computed, and exactly one annihilates random twist points.
gcd(h₁, h₂) = 1 is a property of BLS12-381, not of the family, and it is the
fact the G2 test turns on.

The tool uses its own arithmetic, not the library's, and asks **@noble/curves**
— which shares no code with this implementation — for its own torsion test on
the generators, random multiples, (0, 2), lifted points, cofactor points and
mixtures. All three answers agree on every point.

**The G2 test is nearly free, and the reason generalises.** A Miller loop over Q
starts T at Q and runs the bits of |x| through it — a doubling per bit, an
addition per set bit — so when the loop ends, T *is* [|x|]Q, computed by exactly
the witnessed ladder a standalone test would emit. Checking B is therefore a
comparison against a value the verifier already has: T = −ψ(B), four
congruences and one conjugate-and-scale, 389 bytes including the twist equation.
The same holds for any spender-supplied G2 point that enters a pairing — σ in a
BLS signature is another. We have not seen this observation stated, and have not
searched for it.

It carries a precondition that no module can check about itself: T must be the
loop's T, never a value the spender supplied. Both verifiers take it from their
own loop, `npm test` asserts that no split stage takes in a final running point,
and the fault-isolation test below gives stage 2 a B outside G2 with the check
and without it.

**The G1 test is two ladders by |x|.** |x| has six set bits, so [x²]P as
[|x|]([|x|]P) is 136 point operations, where one ladder by x² would be about 190
and [r]P about 380. The scalar is a compile-time constant, so no step branches.
`g1.inSubgroup` is 8,517 bytes with its 136 witnessed inverses, and it contains
the curve equation for a reason worth stating: short-Weierstrass doubling and
addition never read b. Given coordinates off y² = x³ + 4, the ladder runs on
whichever y² = x³ + b′ they lie on — a curve with its own φ and its own group
order, on which the argument above also goes through. Without the curve check
the module would certify order r on a curve nobody asked about.

| `g1.inSubgroup`, standalone | 8,517 |
| `g2.inSubgroup`, standalone | 389 |
| the Groth16 verifier without subgroup checks | 1,229,196 |
| with them | 1,246,986 |
| **the checks** | **17,790 — 1.4%** |

The previous revision priced these as ladders at 5–10% of the verifier. The G2
half turned out to be a comparison, and the G1 half cheaper than a ladder by the
choice of scalar.

**Which check refuses is tested, not inferred.** The whole verifier refuses a
proof with A outside G1 for two reasons at once — the subgroup test, and a
pairing equation that no longer holds — so the whole verifier cannot say which
check did the work. A split stage can (§8.3): stage 1 checks no equation at all.
Stages 1 and 2 are each built twice, with the subgroup checks and without, and
given A + (0, 2) — (0, 2) has order 3, so this is on the curve and of order 3r —
and B + [r]R for a twist point R. Without the checks both stages accept and carry
the point forward; with them both refuse.

The verifying key is checked once, off chain, when the coin is built: a key with
α, β, γ, δ or any ICᵢ outside its subgroup does not compile.

### 8.1 Systems findings

Scripts of this size exposed two wallet defects that had never mattered at a few
hundred bytes.

**Coin selection inherits ancestry.** Outputs were taken in indexer order, which
swept up dust from earlier deployments — and that dust sits at the *end* of the
unconfirmed chain those deployments created. A single fresh 50,000-satoshi
output funds a 334 KB transaction alone; funded by three satoshis of dust first,
the same transaction inherited fourteen links of ancestry and was rejected with
`too-long-mempool-chain`. The fee was never the problem.

**Fee estimation must be script-derived.** Funding stopped at a fixed
`amount + 5000` constant — reasonable for a 400-byte covenant, meaningless for a
script whose fee alone is 33,389 satoshis. It did not underfund; it swept the
wallet, which is how the first defect acquired its dust.

Both are ordinary bugs. We report them because they are the kind of assumption
that only very large scripts falsify, and anyone repeating this work will meet
them.

## 8.2 Decomposing across a transaction

A pairing in one script is 817,031 bytes against a 500,000-byte policy, and the
gap is not closable by optimisation. It is closable by putting the composition
somewhere other than the script.

```
input 0   the Miller loop            publishes f as an OP_RETURN output
input 1   the final exponentiation   consumes that same output
```

Both inputs of a spend observe the same `hashOutputs`. If each requires that
commitment to be the data output *it* constructs, the two must have constructed
identical bytes — enforced by the transaction rather than assumed. Neither
script contains the other's code, which is the necessary property: a covenant
can only commit to a successor whose bytes it can build, and a 344 KB script
cannot carry a 475 KB one.

The argument is short enough to give in full. Write M for the Miller loop, F for
the final exponentiation, and C(s) for "the transaction's output set is exactly
the data output carrying s". The two predicates are

```
P₁(x, s) = [ s = M(x) ] ∧ C(s)
P₂(s, y) = [ y = F(s) ] ∧ C(s)
```

A transaction spending both inputs satisfies P₁(x, s₀) and P₂(s₁, y) for the s₀
and s₁ each input used. C is a function of the transaction alone, so C(s₀) and
C(s₁) hold of the same output set; since C determines s (the output *is* s, and
`hashOutputs` is collision-resistant), s₀ = s₁ = s. Therefore

```
y = F(s) = F(M(x)) = e(P, Q)
```

and the transaction's validity is exactly the statement that the pairing was
computed correctly. Nothing in the argument mentions pairings: it holds for any
f = F ∘ M with a cut point whose state is small enough to publish.

| | lock | unlock | verdict |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| input 0, `pairing.publish` | 343,799 | 350,922 | accepted |
| input 1, `pairing.consume` | 475,017 | 479,302 | accepted |
| output, `OP_RETURN` | 588 | — | twelve Fp12 coefficients |

Both inputs are verified by `bsv.Script.Interpreter` under relay policy flags,
**and the transaction is on mainnet**: funding `92bb3f0e…`, spend `fd0f553e…`,
164,997 satoshis. What the network establishes, in one transaction, is that the
Miller loop ran correctly on P and Q, that its twelve outputs were published,
that the same twelve were consumed, and that their final exponentiation is
e(P, Q).

**Joint grinding.** OP_PUSH_TX requires each preimage to satisfy a canonical
low-S condition, ordinarily obtained by grinding that input's `nSequence`. But
`hashSequence` commits to *every* input's sequence, so grinding input 0
invalidates input 1's preimage and conversely: independent grinds do not
compose. The search is over the pair. About one preimage in fifty passes, so a
pair lands in a few thousand attempts — 733 in the reported run. Rebuilding a
preimage carrying a 344 KB `scriptCode` that many times is unnecessary: only 36
bytes change, so each preimage is constructed once and `hashSequence` and
`nSequence` are patched in place, then checked against the ones the library
produces.

**A constraint this surfaces.** OP_PUSH_TX's preimage *contains the script it
unlocks*, so a covenant's unlocking script is as large as its locking script.
`pairing.consume` locks in 475,017 bytes and unlocks in 479,302 — 20,698 under
the policy. For covenants of this size the binding limit is the **unlocking**
script, not the locking one, which is not where one would look for it.

**What the shared commitment does not establish.** The argument above is sound
about the bytes and silent about the input set: C(s) constrains what the
transaction publishes, not which inputs are in it. A stage spent alone satisfies
its covenant vacuously, and for P₂ that is exploitable rather than merely
untidy. F is exponentiation by d = 3(p¹²−1)/r; the target e(P,Q) has order r;
gcd(d, r) = 1. So f = e(P,Q)^(d⁻¹ mod r) satisfies [y = F(f)] for two modular
exponentiations and no Miller loop. We carry this out against the interpreter
and the coin is accepted. No value is transferred — the covenant forbids change
— but a reader who checks one input concludes a computation occurred that did
not, and an adversary can consume the coin ahead of the honest spend and prevent
the real one from ever being published.

The remedy is the field the construction never read. `hashPrevouts` is the
double-SHA of every spent outpoint in order, each a 32-byte txid followed by a
4-byte little-endian index. The stages of one computation are outputs of a
single funding transaction, so the entire list is a function of that one txid:
witness 32 bytes, rebuild the list in script, require the hash to equal the
preimage's `hashPrevouts`, and require this input's own outpoint — at offset 68
— to be the slot the script claims. The predicate becomes

```
P_i(s_{i-1}, s_i) = [ s_i = F_i(s_{i-1}) ] ∧ C(B) ∧ S_i
```

with S_i asserting that the spend has exactly N inputs, all outputs of one
transaction at indices 0..N−1, and that this is input i. It costs 47 bytes at
two stages and 56 at three. What remains outside the script is that those
outputs carry the scripts they are supposed to — an outpoint does not name a
script — which is one check on one funding transaction rather than a judgement
about the spend. All three stages of §8.3 bind their siblings; the deployed
two-way instance predates the finding and is reported with it.

This generalises past pairings. Any computation too large for one script can be
cut at a point where the intermediate state is small, with each piece requiring
the same commitment to that state. The state here is 588 bytes for a computation
of 817,031, and the cut is where the two stages of the pairing already meet.

## 8.3 Three ways, and a cut with no natural seam

The two-way decomposition cut a pairing where its own structure already divided
it. A Groth16 verifier does not offer that convenience. It is 1,246,986 bytes,
and its Miller loop — three pairings already sharing one accumulator — is

```
705,838 bytes
```

on its own. That is 205,838 over the policy *before* the final exponentiation is
considered. Cutting at the M/F boundary yields a piece that still does not fit,
and no rebalancing of two stages can fix it: the loop must itself be cut.

It is cut at round 31 of 63. Nothing distinguishes round 31 — it is not a seam
in the mathematics, only the point at which the two halves of the loop are each
small enough. What makes an arbitrary cut admissible is that the state there is
small: the accumulator f, twelve Fp coefficients, and the running point T of
each of the three pairings, four coefficients apiece. Twenty-four values.

The subgroup checks of §7.6 are placed where each is cheapest. Stage 1 checks A
and C into G1 before its half of the loop. B cannot be checked there — at round
31 its running point is [|x| ≫ 32]B, not [|x|]B — so stage 2 checks it after the
loop ends. The running point stage 2 *resumes* from is a witness, but the blob
pins it to the one stage 1 computed, so the one it finishes with is [|x|]B.

| | lock | unlock | verdict |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| input 0, A, C ∈ G1; rounds 1–31; publishes S₁ | 399,402 | 424,186 | accepted |
| input 1, rounds 32–63; B ∈ G2; publishes S₂ | 371,834 | 383,299 | accepted |
| input 2, final exponentiation vs e(α, β) | 475,647 | 481,527 | accepted |
| output, `OP_RETURN` | 2,156 | — | 44 field elements |

The subgroup checks account for 16,849 bytes of stage 1 and 957 of stage 2.
Each stage bounds only the blob's values at its entry — the proof and the two cut
states, which are compared as bytes — and leaves every inverse to the module that
reads it; the audit checks by provenance that every numeric witness of every
stage is still bounded (§10.3).

All three are verified by `bsv.Script.Interpreter` under relay policy flags. The
transaction is 1,291,329 bytes and establishes, as a single atomic object, that
a Groth16 proof of the statement *"the holder was at least 21 years old as of
2026"* — generated by snarkjs, not by us (§7.5) — verifies, with its points in
their subgroups.

**And it is on mainnet.** `bin/deploy-groth16.js`
built the funding transaction and the spend with the same code that produced the
figures above, ran all three inputs through the interpreter against the real
funding txid, and only then broadcast them:

```
funding  025f20f1d4156aa354afef37b1ec67e5c461f11375bc739902845a3b985dc5f0
spend    cad6d2cca44fffb2f445009f392b668382d79d120b1c14db0fd4a8d192204bb6
```

The funding transaction is 1,247,999 bytes and carries the three coins at outputs
0, 1 and 2; the spend is 1,291,329 bytes and paid 129,133 satoshis, 253,934 for
the two together at 100 sat/kB. Its nLockTime grind landed at 216,991.

The funding transaction was mined at once, in block 966,795. **The spend was
not**, and how it eventually reached a block is the most useful thing in this
section.

It was accepted into WhatsOnChain's node's mempool and went no further. Five
later blocks from five different miners left it out; a second indexer never saw
it; and submitting it to GorillaPool's ARC returned `REJECTED` with the node's
reason, **`too-long-validation-time`**. It stayed unmined for 128 blocks. The
operator's explanation, when asked, was that their node gives a peer-relayed
transaction at most a second to validate — `maxnonstdtxvalidationduration`, 1000
ms by default — and this one exceeded it under load. Measured in the JavaScript
interpreter, its three inputs take 4.2 seconds between them against 2.7 for the
two inputs of the §8.2 spend, which relayed and was mined: 1.5 times as long,
with no input individually heavier than §8.2's. The pool then submitted it to
their own node directly, and mined it in **block 966,923**.

So the binding constraint moved a third time, and changed kind. The script-size
policy bound the single script, which is why the computation was split; the
unlocking script's size bound each stage, because OP_PUSH_TX's preimage contains
the script it unlocks; and validation time binds the *transaction* — which is
exactly the unit the decomposition put all three stages into.

Unlike the first two, this one is not a property of the transaction alone. The
spend is valid, and the block containing it is valid; what refused it was the
budget a node applies to work arriving from a peer. A transaction may therefore
be consensus-valid, relayable nowhere, and mined anyway by an operator willing to
take it by hand. We report it as mined because it is, and record how, because
"it confirmed" would leave out the part a reader would need: at this validation
cost, propagation cannot be relied on. A construction that does not depend on
anyone's goodwill has to spread the stages across transactions rather than
inputs, so that no single transaction carries more than a second of validation —
a different covenant, with its own soundness argument.

**The soundness argument, for N stages.** Write the computation as
f = F_N ∘ … ∘ F_1, let s_i be the state after stage i, and let B be the single
data output carrying every s_i at once. Stage i's predicate is

```
P_i(s_{i-1}, s_i) = [ s_i = F_i(s_{i-1}) ] ∧ C(B)
```

where C(B) is again "the output set is exactly the data output B". Each stage
computes its own transition and *witnesses* every other state in B. A witness is
free to be wrong; what removes the freedom is that C is a function of the
transaction alone, so all N inputs see the same B, and B determines every s_i.
Stage i's witnessed s_{i-1} is therefore the same s_{i-1} that stage i−1
*computed* and was checked against. Composing along the chain gives
s_N = f(s_0), and the transaction's validity is that statement.

The N-stage case differs from N = 2 in one respect worth stating: each stage
carries the *whole* blob, not merely its own two endpoints, because C commits to
the output in full. This is why the blob is kept small — 44 field elements,
2,156 bytes, for a computation of 1,246,986 — and why the cut point is chosen by
the size of the state it exposes rather than by the sizes of the pieces alone.

**Triple grinding, and choosing the right field to grind.** One preimage in
fifty is canonical (measured: 2.01%), so a pair lands in ~2,500 attempts and a
**triple in ~123,000**. The two-way construction ground `nSequence`; at three
inputs that becomes untenable, and for a reason specific to BIP-143. `nSequence`
appears *twice* in a preimage: as its own field near the end, and inside
`hashSequence` at offset 36. Moving it changes bytes 36 onward, so every
SHA-256 block of a ~400 KB preimage must be recomputed — 123,000 times, three
preimages each.

`nLockTime` appears **once**, eight bytes from the end. Grinding it instead
leaves every SHA-256 block but the last unchanged, so each input's midstate is
computed once and copied per attempt, and the per-attempt cost falls from
hashing 400 KB to hashing 64 bytes. The reported run tried 168,127 nLockTimes in
**2.5 seconds**; 3,451 cleared input 0, 62 cleared inputs 0 and 1, one cleared
all three. Small nLockTime values are block heights already past, so the
transaction remains final — the search stops well short of the chain height, and
the deployment refuses a result that is not below it. The fast filter is checked
against the library's own canonicalisation on every candidate it considers before
being trusted.

This is a case of the paper's general theme arriving somewhere unexpected. The
question "which transaction field should carry the nonce?" has no answer in
cryptography and no answer in isolation; it is answered by where the field sits
relative to a hash function's block boundaries, which is exactly the kind of
target-specific fact §9.1 argues should be delegated to a search rather than
decided in advance.

## 8.4 One stage per transaction, because relay is the binding constraint

§8.3's spend could not travel, and the remedy follows from what refused it.
Validation time bounds the TRANSACTION, so the unit of work has to become the
unit of relay: one stage per transaction, with the state passed along a chain of
small coins rather than between the inputs of one.

The carrier coin holds a PAIR of states, `prev` and `cur`, and requires its
successor to be the same script with **its own cur as the successor's prev**,
leaving that successor's cur unconstrained. The stage coin beside it requires the
successor's cur to be what it computed from the prev it consumed:

```
carrier:  output = body ‖ myCur ‖ X       X witnessed, free
stage:    output = body ‖ p     ‖ F(p)    p witnessed, free
```

Both hash the same output, so p = myCur and X = F(p). Neither reads the other's
script — an outpoint does not name one — and they agree because the bytes they
each rebuild are the same bytes.

| | lock | unlock | validates in |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| tx₁, the Miller loop | 344,840 | 351,963 | 1,351 ms |
| tx₂, the exponentiation and the carrier | 476,067 | 480,352 | 1,679 ms |
| §8.2's two-input spend, which relayed | — | — | 2,749 ms |
| §8.3's three-input spend, which did not | — | — | 4,219 ms |

The heaviest link asks for 0.61 of what relayed and 0.40 of what did not. The
milliseconds are this machine's JavaScript interpreter in one idle run and a
node's C++ is far quicker; what transfers is the ratio. It is
on mainnet: funding `2b7ec0a5…`, then `a8ae58b2…` and `0482eccb…`, all three
mined in block 966,954 — by the pool whose relay check had refused §8.3's spend,
and which minutes earlier refused this chain's first link for paying two satoshis
under its size-derived minimum. That was our arithmetic error, since fixed; what
it records about the platform is that a node's relay threshold, a miner's mining
threshold and consensus are three different things.

**What it gives up is atomicity.** In §8.3 every stage checked through
hashPrevouts that the spend consumed exactly its siblings, and the transaction's
validity was the whole statement. Here the carrier arrives from the previous
transaction, whose txid nothing could know when the stage coins were written, so
no stage can insist the carrier beside it is genuine. The claim becomes one about
a chain, and a reader completes it by walking one: the funding transaction's
outputs carry the stage scripts, and each link spends the coin and the carrier it
should. `npm run verify:chainwalk` performs exactly that walk against the chain,
and compares the value the last carrier holds with the pairing computed here.

**Headroom.** Stage 3 unlocks in 481,527 bytes — 18,473 under the policy, again
in the unlocking script rather than the locking one. A four-way split would
relieve it, and the construction admits one without modification.

## 9. An inverted cost model

Conventional elliptic-curve implementation assumes I ≫ M and chooses projective
or Jacobian coordinates to eliminate inversions, accepting several extra
multiplications per step.

Under witness-assisted verification, an Fp2 inversion is one Fp2 multiplication
and two range checks. Empirically I_witnessed ≈ 4M, so the trade reverses:

> **affine > projective**

The Miller loop here is affine throughout, and projective formulas would make it
larger. We state this as the paper's most transferable claim:

> The optimal cryptographic algorithm depends on the verifier's computational
> model. A witness-assisted stack machine has a different cost hierarchy from a
> processor, and received implementation wisdom does not transfer unexamined.

Three further consequences of the same principle appear above. Multiplying by
zero is free on a processor and costs bytes in a script (§5.3). An LSB-first
ladder's multiplication-by-one is free at runtime and costs 3,110 bytes when
unrolled — the marginal cost of an Fp12 product inside a composition (§5,
Table 1). And compressed cyclotomic squaring, whose decompression
inversion normally confines it to long squaring chains, pays immediately here
(§6.1) — the same reversal, arrived at independently.

## 9.1 Instruction selection: letting the target decide

Stating "affine beats projective in Script" is a finding about one construction.
Making it *operational* is a different thing, and the repository does: given
several modules that compute the same mathematical function, `select()` proves
they agree and then chooses by a stated objective over the cost vector
(locking bytes, unlocking bytes, total, opcodes, peak stack depth, fee).

```
f ↦ f² in Fp12            lock     unlock      total   opcodes  stack
  fp12.sqr               2,292        588      2,880     1,540     37
  fp12.cycSqr            1,259        588      1,847       968     34  ◀

f ↦ f^|x| in Fp12
  fp12.powX             98,902        588     99,490    71,954     61
  fp12.powXc            73,582      1,175     74,757    54,204     71  ◀
```

Nothing there was told that the cyclotomic identities or the compression win.
Both were emitted and counted. This is the smallest honest form of an
instruction-selection pass over mathematics: the same function, several
lowerings, and a target-specific cost model choosing among them — which is what
turns §9's observation from an assertion into a mechanism.

The `total` column is where a witnessed construction can lose. `fp12.powXc`
buys 25,320 bytes of locking script for 587 bytes of unlocking script; a
construction with a worse ratio would be rejected on the same evidence, and the
objective is a flag rather than an assumption.

### The problem being solved

Stated formally, the selector solves

```
P* = argmin  C(P)
     P ∈ 𝒫(f, D)
```

where 𝒫(f, D) is the set of Script programs *proven* equivalent to the
mathematical function f over the domain D, and C is the measured cost vector
(locking bytes, unlocking bytes, total, opcodes, peak stack depth, fee). Both
qualifiers carry weight. The domain D is part of the problem, not a footnote:
`fp12.cycSqr` belongs to 𝒫(square, G_Φ6) and not to 𝒫(square, Fp12), and a
selector that ignores D returns a wrong answer confidently. And membership of
𝒫 is *established*, not assumed — see below.

Witnesses widen 𝒫 rather than merely changing costs within it. A program need
not compute f; it need only satisfy

```
∃w : P(x, w) = 1  ⟺  f(x) = 1
```

so a verification relation cheaper than any evaluation of f is admissible. The
witnessed inverse is exactly that: `a·z ≡ 1 (mod p)` with z supplied is not a
cheaper way to compute an inverse, it is a different program that certifies one.
This is why the cost model inverts (§9) rather than merely shifting — the
feasible set is not the same set.

The pipeline this implies is a compiler back end:

```
        mathematical predicate
                 │
                 ▼
             typed IR
                 │
          ┌──────┴──────┐
          ▼             ▼
      lowering A    lowering B
          │             │
          └──────┬──────┘
                 │
        semantic agreement over D
                 │
                 ▼
            cost vectors
                 │
                 ▼
          target selection
                 │
                 ▼
          stack scheduling
                 │
                 ▼
           Bitcoin Script
```

Everything above the dashed middle exists here in embryo: the modules are the
lowerings, `requires`/`ensures` are the typed IR's facts, `select()` is the
agreement-and-choice pass, and the assembler is the scheduler. What is not yet
built is a front end that takes a formula and produces the lowerings, and a
scheduler that does register allocation rather than following the order a human
wrote.

**The agreement step is the contribution, not the ranking.** A selector that
picks the cheaper of two implementations without first establishing that they
compute the same thing is a benchmark attached to a bug. And comparing their
*models* is insufficient — we found this by trying it. `fp12.cycSqr`'s model is
the general square, correct on all of Fp12; only its emitted script is confined
to the cyclotomic subgroup. Two candidates whose models agree can have scripts
that do not. The check therefore verifies each candidate's script against its
own model on every vector, and the models against each other:

```
script_i = model_i = model_j = script_j
```

Run off the subgroup the same two candidates are refused rather than ranked, and
that refusal is itself a test case. Agreement is always agreement *on a domain*,
and each contest records the one its vectors came from; "cheaper" without "and
equivalent, here" is not a result.

## 10. Correctness methodology

1. **Independent reference.** The BigInt implementation agrees with
   `@noble/curves` on all twelve Fp12 coefficients — not merely on bilinearity,
   which a wrong pairing can also satisfy. An early line function here was
   non-degenerate, produced order-r values, passed every component test, and was
   wrong; byte equality caught it. Eight chosen scalar pairs, three boundary
   pairs (1 and r−1 in each position), and twelve generated from a printed seed:
   320 assertions in all.
2. **Model versus emitter.** Each module's specification is different code from
   its schedule.
3. **The real interpreter**, under relay policy flags.
4. **Adversarial witnesses**, on every run: 1,367 forged witnesses refused.
5. **Published vectors** where they exist (RFC 6238, BIP-340), and the host
   library's own secp256k1 and Merkle implementations.
6. **Randomised differential testing of every module** against its own model,
   on inputs generated from the module's stated domain rather than chosen: 47
   modules, none disagreeing. The four modules whose domain is a *subgroup* —
   which an interval cannot state — carry generators that produce elements of
   it, so those rounds test the module where it is defined rather than reporting
   a disagreement that is the module telling the truth.
7. **Boundary values**: 0, 1, p − 1, and both coefficients at p − 1.
8. **Byte-exact chain reconstruction** of every deployed script, coin by coin
   for deployments of several coins.
9. **Generated figures.** Every number in Table 1–8 is produced by measurement.
10. **Computed number theory.** The facts the subgroup tests rest on are
    computed by `npm run subgroup` with arithmetic independent of the library's,
    and the tests' verdicts are checked against an independent implementation's
    (§7.6).
11. **Refusals by the interpreter.** A refusal case is counted only when the
    script refused; a case whose spend cannot be built is a failure (§10.4).

### 10.1 Reproducibility of the numbers, not only of the artifact

Three documents in this repository quoted measured figures in prose and all
three had drifted — one claimed an RSA verifier was 955 bytes when it was 969,
another that a curve addition was 139 bytes when a soundness fix had made it 167.

Measurements are therefore generated:

```
source → measurement → results.json → tables → this paper
```

rather than `source → someone remembers → a number in a PDF`. The test suite
fails if `results.json` disagrees with the code, and if `tables.md` disagrees
with `results.json`.

The figures for the three-way split were the exception until this revision:
copied into `results.json` from a run of the tool that produced them, and
therefore regenerated by nothing. They are now produced by building the spend
inside the generator and verifying all three inputs before measuring it.

## 10.2 Related work

**A note on this section.** The references below are the standard results this
implementation is built on and are cited because it uses them. The *positioning*
claim — that lowering pairing arithmetic to a general-purpose script language's
integer and stack primitives has not been done this way before — has not been
established by a systematic literature search, and is stated as an impression
rather than a result. It must be settled before submission.

**Pairings and their computation.** Miller's algorithm [1] is the loop; the
BLS family of pairing-friendly curves is due to Barreto, Lynn and Scott [2], and
BLS12-381 in particular to Bowe [3], now specified in the CFRG's
pairing-friendly-curves draft [4]. The loop used here is the optimal ate pairing
[5]. The final exponentiation's cyclotomic structure follows Granger and
Scott [6], and the compressed representation of §6.1 is a variant of the
compression Karabina introduced [7] — arrived at here by reading [6]'s
identities in the Fp4 basis rather than by following [7], and differing from it
in which two coefficients are dropped.

**Subgroup membership.** Endomorphism-based membership tests for BLS12-381 are
due to Bowe [12]; the forms φ(P) = [−x²]P and ψ(Q) = [x]Q used in §7.6 are
Scott's [13]. The conditions they rest on were computed for this curve rather
than taken from either.

**The proof system.** Groth16 [8] is the verifier of §7.

**Implementation cost models.** The projective-coordinate practice that §9
inverts is standard throughout the pairing-implementation literature; Beuchat
et al. [9] and Aranha et al. [10] are representative of the optimisation
strategies assumed there, all of which take a processor's I ≫ M as given.

**The platform.** Bitcoin Script's transaction-introspection technique depends
on the BIP-143 sighash structure [11], which post-Genesis Bitcoin SV retains and
whose arbitrary-precision arithmetic makes any of this possible.

**What is not cited, and should be.** Verifier implementations on other
blockchain virtual machines — Ethereum's alt-bn128 precompiles, zkSync, and the
various Solidity Groth16 verifiers — occupy the adjacent space and rely on
native pairing operations or on a VM designed with the arithmetic in mind. A
proper comparison against them belongs here and has not been written.

## 10.3 An adversarial audit of our own assumptions

`npm run audit` asks the four questions a reviewer would ask, structurally where
possible, and reports what does not hold as prominently as what does. Five of
its findings changed the implementation.

**A stage was not bound to its siblings, and an attack follows.** The audit's
third section asks whether two inputs can disagree about f, and the answer is
no. The question it was not asking is whether one input can proceed without the
other, and the answer to that was yes: `hashOutputs` is checked and
`hashPrevouts` is not. Section 8.2 gives the forgery — f = e(P,Q)^(d⁻¹ mod r),
two modexps — and `npm run attack:siblings` runs it against the interpreter,
where the coin is accepted. The fix reads `hashPrevouts` for 47 bytes at two
stages and 56 at three, and is used by every stage of §8.3. The instance already
on chain cannot be changed and is reported as it stands; the demonstration is
kept as a standing test rather than retired once fixed, because what it protects
is a property of the *transaction*, which no single script can assert alone.

This one is worth dwelling on as a methodological point. The soundness argument
in §8.2 is correct — we did not misprove it. It establishes what it says, which
is a statement about bytes, and we read it as a statement about the computation.
Every test we had ran the honest two-input transaction, so nothing exercised the
gap. The audit found it only once it was asked a question phrased in terms of
what an adversary omits rather than what they substitute.

**Witness canonicity, exhaustively.** The test kit attacks witnesses, and
samples once a module has 136 of them. Sampling cannot establish "every witness
is bounded"; only structure can. Each stack slot now carries the input it
descends from through picks, rolls and renames, and every bound records that
origin, so the question is answered for all **1,970 numeric witnesses** — the
library's and every stage of the three-way split — at once rather than for a
sample. The audit grants a range only to inputs that are not witnesses, so a
witness's bound has to come from something the script does.

The first run reported `ec.add` and `ec.double` as unbounded. They were not:
they bounded their witnessed inverse with a hand-written `OP_WITHIN` that the
recorder could not see. That is worth stating because the fix was not to
special-case them — a bound nothing can observe is a bound an audit reports as
missing, so they were rewritten to go through the same call everything else
uses, which emits identical bytes and is visible. Every deployed script still
reconstructs byte for byte. Bounds the framework places from a module's
`requires` are recorded the same way.

**A gap in the attack generator, found the same way.** Chasing that false
positive exposed a true one: the kit's default attacks look for the modulus
under `params.n`, and the elliptic-curve modules call it `p`. So the
*same-residue* attacks — the ones that test canonicity rather than soundness,
and the ones the earlier four bugs needed — were silently skipped for every
`ec.*` module. Those modules had been passing an attack suite that never asked
them the question. Fixed, and they pass it now.

**The proof points were not checked to be on their curves**, and then **not
checked to be in their subgroups**. The first was fixed in the previous revision
at a few hundred bytes. The second stood as this audit's most significant open
finding, priced at 5–10% of the verifier and left open on the grounds that the
endomorphism tests had to be derived and verified rather than recalled. §7.6 is
that derivation. The audit now checks that β and ψ's constants are in the
locking script, that A and C each carry a whole G1 ladder, that each subgroup
module accepts a subgroup point and refuses the same point shifted by a cofactor
element, and that a verifying key with a point outside its subgroup does not
compile.

## 10.4 A refusal that never reached the interpreter

A refusal case asserts that a module rejects an input whatever the spender
supplies. The kit built such a case's unlocking script from the values the case
named, inside the same `try` as the evaluation — so a case that named the proof
and not its witnesses threw while *pushing* an undefined value, and the throw was
reported as a refusal.

Every refusal case on the whole Groth16 verifier named only the proof. So "a
displaced A is refused", "a point off the curve is refused", "a proof from
someone underage is refused" and "a valid proof is refused by a coin for a
different statement" had been reported, in `tools/groth16.js`,
`tools/groth16-external.js` and the demonstration, without the interpreter ever
running them. Cases on smaller modules were unaffected because they pin their
witnesses, which is why the defect survived: every refusal it hid was one that
was also expected to be true.

It was found while adding the subgroup cases, by reading the kit rather than by
a test failing. The kit now fills a refusal case's unnamed witnesses from the
module's hint — which is the stronger test anyway, every inverse correct for the
points supplied so that nothing fails until the property does — and a case that
still cannot be built is a failure. A ninth deliberately broken module in the
kit's self-test is a refusal case that omits a witness for an input the module
accepts, and the kit is required to report it. Every affected case has since been
run by the interpreter, and every one still refuses.

## 11. Limitations

- **A complete pairing has not executed on mainnet in a single script** and
  cannot at 817,031 bytes under a 500,000-byte script policy. It has executed
  across two inputs of one transaction, which is a different and weaker
  statement about script size and an identical one about what was computed.
- **The three-way Groth16 spend did not propagate.** It is mined (§8.3), but the
  relay path refused it for its validation time and a pool took it by direct
  submission; nothing about it should be read as evidence that a transaction of
  this validation cost will reach a miner by itself.
- **`fp12.powX` is deployed but superseded.** It computes a correct f^|x| and
  the network executed it; `fp12.powXc` replaced it, so it is not a component of
  the pairing measured here and its bytes are not counted as such.
- **The Groth16 verifier is for a fixed statement.** Spend-time public inputs
  need on-chain scalar multiplication, priced at about 40,000 bytes per input
  but not emitted.
- **The G2 subgroup test has a precondition no module can check about itself**:
  that the running point it compares against is the Miller loop's own. It is
  tested structurally and by fault isolation (§7.6), not proven from inside the
  module.
- **Groth16's own soundness is out of scope**, as is the setup that produced a
  verifying key. The verifier is tested against proofs from an independent
  proving stack (§7.5) and against a fixture built from the group law (§7.4);
  neither says anything about whether Groth16 is sound, only about whether this
  checks its verification equation on points of the right groups.
- **One proving stack, two circuits.** Nothing here depends on circuit size — the
  verifier's cost is ℓ public inputs and three pairings regardless — but the
  claim rests on proofs from one prover.
- **`fp12.cycSqr` is correct only on the cyclotomic subgroup**, a precondition
  the interval-based contract system cannot express. It is discharged
  structurally by the easy part, and stated as a note rather than enforced.
- **Policy, not consensus.** The 500,000-byte boundary is a relay-policy number.
- **One implementation of the compiler.** The measurements are of this emitter;
  a better one would produce smaller scripts.
- **The novelty claim is unestablished.** §10.2 says what this is built on. It
  does not say what has been done before, because that has not been searched
  properly, and no priority claim should be read into its absence.

## 12. Roadmap

The final exponentiation is 57.9% of a pairing and is still where remaining
effort belongs, though §6.1 has taken it below the policy boundary. Better
addition chains, further witness-assisted subcomputations and transaction-level
decomposition across several outputs are all open. Whether a whole pairing can
be brought under the boundary in one script is an engineering question, not an
expressiveness one. Bringing the whole composition under the 500,000-byte
boundary would turn the existing transaction-level pairing into a
**single-script** one, removing the need for inter-input state binding and
simplifying deployment to a single coin. That is a further 317,031 bytes, and it
is the remaining milestone rather than the remaining question.

Beyond BLS12-381: BLS signature verification is a two-pairing product; KZG
opening is likewise. Both land directly on the multi-pairing predicate in §7.1,
and in a BLS signature the spender's G2 point σ enters a Miller loop, so its
subgroup test costs a comparison (§7.6). The public key is a G1 point fixed in
the script and checked when the coin is built.

Further out, §9.1 is the seed of something larger. If a target-specific cost
model can choose between two lowerings of a squaring, it can choose between
lowerings of anything, and the interesting object stops being any particular
pairing implementation and becomes the compiler: a mathematical predicate in,
a locking script out, with the choice of representation, the placement of
witnesses and the stack schedule all decided by measurement against this target
rather than inherited from a processor's. The results here suggest that such a
compiler would make materially different choices from a conventional one, and
that those choices are discoverable rather than needing to be known in advance.

## 13. Conclusion

Bitcoin Script has no pairing opcode, no extension-field type, and no inversion
primitive. It has arbitrary-precision multiplication and modular reduction, and
a complete BLS12-381 pairing was nonetheless evaluated by the network — through
two transaction-bound predicates, one computing the Miller loop and publishing
its Fp12 result, the other consuming that committed result and performing the
final exponentiation. Each stage was also deployed and spent on its own. A
Groth16 verifier for a fixed statement is 1,246,986 bytes with its proof points
checked into their subgroups, validated against the interpreter on proofs from
an independent prover, and evaluated by the network across three inputs of one transaction, which reached a block only because a pool accepted it directly. The prices are measured rather than argued about.

The general lesson is not about pairings. It is that shifting computation into
prover-supplied witnesses, and verifying algebraic identities instead of
recomputing values, changes which algorithm is optimal. The absence of an opcode
is not the absence of the computation; it is a cost, and costs can be measured.
